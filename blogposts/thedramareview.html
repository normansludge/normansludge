Movie Review: The Drama

Forgiveness? What are you insane?

In my never ending efforts to try and get out of the house, I decided to spend the whopping $3.50 price for a ticket to see The Drama in a real-life movie theater. I’m about two months late to the party, but there is a reason the tickets were that cheap. From all that I’ve heard (which isn’t that much outside of my immediate friends who haven’t seen the movie and are merely summarizing what they’ve heard) the movie was just “okay” or outright “not good.” I went in having no clue what the movie was even about, but I knew that $3.50 to watch Robert Pattinson play a handsome, socially-awkward museum art curator on a big screen was an absolute bargain.

The movie was absolutely mortifying to watch. I somehow forgot just how much I find second-hand embarrassment to be absolutely the most torturous thing that can be committed to film, but after realizing I quite literally had my hands over my eyes, curled up into a little ball in my theatre seat, through some scenes was an unpleasant reminder to my fears. The movie, in my opinion, is good. For superficial reasons of it’s goodness I can claim that Robert Pattinson once again continues to shock me with his range (especially having seen The Lighthouse earlier this year), Zendaya’s incredibly ambiguous performance as both sympathetic and alien is the linchpin of the entire story and she nails that balance masterfully, and, most importantly, that goddamn apartment is gorgeous.

Far less superficially, I am particularly enamored with one scene in the movie (spoilers ahead).

In a basic synopsis of necessary context: Emma (Zendaya) drunkenly admits that she had fully planned out and nearly executed a school shooting when she was younger. The table of drunken friends she admits this to—consisting of her husband Charlie (Pattinson), the best man Mike, and maid of honor Rachel—were horrified. Rachel finds this confession uniquely monstrous given that her cousin was paralyzed as a result of a shooting and spends the rest of the movie being extremely hostile. The story then follows Pattinson trying to work through how the woman he loves could have ever been capable of (though only capable of, as she is innocent) imagining such unimaginable cruelty.

My favorite part of the movie is when Charlie makes an effort to appeal to Rachel that Emma is a changed person. In a darkly funny scene, Charlie makes up a fake story about Emma seeing her best friend die in a horrible car crash at the perfectly traumatizeable age of ten. In reality, this was Emma’s neighbor who was several years older than her and they weren’t even friends; she had only heard the news of the accident. But Rachel, who had been so steadfastly against Emma leading up to that point, took a brief pause of consideration. The more dubious parts of Charlie’s story destroy that consideration and the movie moves on with Rachel continuing to be steadfastly against Emma. But still, there was a pause.

Is trauma really the necessary boundary between what we consider evil and what we consider sympathetic? Trauma is a pathological diagnosis and, like other pathological diagnoses (ADHD, OCD, depression, autism etc.), is becoming increasingly[1] common. In a recent article I read in The Jacobin, Megan Erickson makes a case against the ever-increasing amount of therapy due to the inherent narcissism of constantly looking inwards to find diagnosable issues instead of outwards at a world that is broken and fully capable of being rebuilt and fixed. This understanding of psychology isn’t new, especially in leftist critiques, and yet I feel as though there is something else in the critique of the therapy generation that is not being reconciled. In Erickson’s article, multiple high school and middle school students explained that they felt mental issues were being used as a “conversation piece” and I would be lying if I said that my experience didn’t corroborate that feeling. Erickson goes on to explain that these conversation pieces are a manifestation of fear in a generation that is “growing up in a Hunger Games–style economy” where community and family are destroyed and “being the best ‘you’ you can be is now how you must make your living.” I think this explanation is accurate, but I also think it is still sort of missing out on the parts of pathology that are useful for creating community. I’m obviously just speculating, but I feel a great deal of why this is happening is because it is a good way to alleviate the crushing weight of agency. Being a person is complicated and trying to be good and just all the time is difficult and—as our social sleights are immortalized by the internet, mass communication, surveillance, and have real-life impacts on our material wellbeing—expected. There isn’t much room to make mistakes.

While I don’t think most people are explicitly conscious of this, we understand that explanations of people’s behaviors sort of demand forgiveness. At the heart of this there exists a sort of internal dichotomy: either this person who has wronged me is capital-E-unforgivably-Evil, or there is some explanation for their actions that muddies up such a clear delineation. Explanation and understanding are the bases of empathy. To bring the train of thought to its station: this need for explanation to empathize and forgive is why I think mental illness and therapy are having such a large impact on our social lives. Being able to evoke the explanatory power of science is pretty handy if you’re worried about being judged on moral grounds (and thus having your future livelihood in question) for missing social cues, being a bit of an asshole or a bit controlling sometimes, or just really liking a specific anime or bugs or something. Neurodivergence is a way of explaining behaviors that aren’t socially acceptable, a way to improve yourself personally and economically, while also being a shared experience that offers up the foundations of community. In an economy that labels hobbies and leisure as frivolous play, the moral failing of relaxation is alleviated by the pathological explanations that take the form of, “I have passion for some facet of life because I am autistic.” Mental illness is a treasure trove of explanations for things that are no longer justifiable socially or economically but are still fundamental parts of being human that we choose to label as dysfunction. In this way, our relationship with pathology is not just a negative symptom of fear and our economy, but also a somewhat freeing way to express our humanity in spite of those fears. While we can and should be restructuring the world so it is actually built for humanity, the ever-widening spectrums of neurodivergence are offering an immediate, yet flawed, way to reclaim personhood through a community who uses the tools of science to explain—and thus forgive—themselves for their “failures.”

But what happens when the only mode of “explanation” and the path to empathy and forgiveness and community is through pathology and mental illness? Worse, what happens when that explanation, or any explanation at all—while it exists in theory—is completely inaccessible? Charlie tries to explain Emma through early childhood trauma or feelings of alienation driven by an American culture obsessed with guns and murder, but all those explanations fall short of satisfactory. Instead of saying that this unknowable gap between them destroys their relationship, the end of the movie offers up a somewhat radical forgiveness that doesn’t rely on concrete explanation at all. Charlie, bloodied up after making a variety of bad decisions of his own that culminated in a fist fight at the wedding, reunites with Emma in their favorite diner and they pretend to meet each other for the first time. They exchange names and silly inside jokes about the absurdity of their shared fantasy and they smile and laugh through teary eyes. Despite everything, they chose to forgive each other and to start fresh. Trauma and explanation don’t matter. The only thing that matters is that they understand each other to be fully and completely human and they choose to be responsible for forgiving eachother for all the faults that reality entails.

"Ah ne’er so dire a Thirst of Glory boast,
Nor in the Critick let the Man be lost!
Good-Nature and Good-Sense must ever join;
To err is Humane; to Forgive, Divine"
Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism

I give The Drama 3.5/5 stars.

[^1] BDM has a very funny article against the word “increasingly” that I find really good. TLDR: Don’t make claims about things increasing unless you really know that they are increasing. So, after double checking with myself, yes: I really do mean that these diagnoses are becoming increasingly common since there is data to support this and I immediately cite an article that utilizes said data.
