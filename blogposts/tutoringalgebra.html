Thoughts on Tutoring Algebra

Systemic failures abound in every direction.

Last semester I got a job as an “Instructional Assistant” for ASU’s introductory college algebra course MAT117. Now that everyone is on winter break and I am both comfortably unemployed and out of school, I feel I can finally look back and try to parse all the emotions I felt while working: the simultaneous joy of helping others succeed weighed against the horror of that success being in spite of vast systemic failures. I had a good time, but there is something deeply wrong about the way math is taught and how people are encouraged to learn and I spent a large part of the semester feeling extremely conflicted. Now that I am free to think more deeply about the experience, I want to informally write a review of my experience to make an effort to organize those thoughts on the future of education and my role within it.

The way MAT117 is taught is really good in theory. With the rise of Khan Academy, I remember hearing a lot of arguments about the future of education having everyone learn from a single “best” teacher. Instead of thousands of teachers, there can be just one teacher (a Sal Khan type) who teaches lectures for the course in the absolute optimal way. Students would watch the recorded lecture while teachers would be responsible for personalizing the education to the specific individual needs of students—much more like a tutor than a professor. In this way there is a standardized curriculum and standardized system of instruction that can provide a standardized basis in a subject for all students. This format seems to be the inspiration for MAT117. The course uses an online learning software ALEKS. Students go at their own pace learning two hundred forty five different topics organized into four modules over the course of the semester. Students work on these topics in class for an hour, three times a week, while instructional assistants walk around and answer any questions the student might have on any given topic. The topics are organized beginning with a text-based learning page a set of practice questions where a correct answer is rewarded one point, two correct answers in a row is rewarded two points, and an incorrect answer results in a point being deducted. Students must get three points to move on to the next subject.

Again, in theory I don’t think this is necessarily a terrible way to teach a course—especially a foundational course like algebra for the thousands of students at one of the largest universities in the world. In practice, it is almost impossible to ignore the course’s catastrophic failures. The way ALEKS teaches math seemingly allows students to pass without learning a single thing about math.

Math, at its core, is about building castles in the sky; about taking a base set of unprovable assumptions (called axioms) to their absolute limits using rigorous logic. It is about creating a definition for a thing and then asking “what the hell does this thing do?” There is a reason so many mathematicians use the same words to describe math as those who describe art (“beautiful” being chief among all adjectives). Math is fundamentally a creative endeavor. Very few people ever get to see this beauty. Edward Frenkel, a professor at UC-Berkley, beautifully poses the question, “What if at school you had to take an 'art class' in which you were only taught how to paint a fence?” There are plenty of musings on the beauty of mathematics and I do think we should reform education to do a better job of making math more than instructions on how to paint a fence, but I also understand the practical reality of trying to teach algebra to thousands of students. I can soberly accept that math education teaches people how to paint fences, but even given that goal, ALEKS is still a failure.

Math, regardless of its aesthetic value, is a tool where you can find solutions to problems using logic. College algebra is great since, after learning a few definitions, everything is just the logical application of those definitions. I like to think I am a good tutor and I realized early on that part of being a good tutor is never answering questions. A direct answer is almost never instructional. A series of simple follow-up questions is. Asking questions lets me taking a student through my own thought process, starting with base principles all the way up to the specific question they are trying to answer. Then, hopefully, they will internalize the generalized logic and be able to apply that to any specific application they need. The problem is, after a very quiet two months of the semester with no questions on the foundations, it became very apparent that the vast majority of people had no idea what they were doing in essence or in practice.

A solid example of this phenomena is the idea of domains of functions. In non-rigorous terms, a function is a thing that takes an input and gives a singular output (so no input gives two different outputs) and the domain of a function is the set of stuff that you can input into your function such that you get an output. These definitions are fundamental to algebra and, luckily, are simple and broad enough that they don’t even explicitly involve numbers. For example, imagine a function that tells you the color of objects[1]. Inputting an apple returns an output of red, inputting grass returns an output of green, orange outputs orange, etc. We have been making these functions since we were children assigning “moo” to the cow and “quack” to the duck. For our color example, the domain is described just by the way I defined our color function: “a function that tells you the color of objects.” Our input must be an object. “Apple” and “grass” are valid inputs, but something more nebulous like “hope” does not have a color. In mathematical terms, we would say that objects are in our domain and ideas (and other non-physical things) are outside of our domain.

Functions and the stuff you can do to them are essentially the entire focus of a college algebra course. And yet, despite their quintessential importance, I didn’t have a single student who gave me a correct answer for the question, “what is a function?” The biggest collective struggle for the class was learning how to find the domains of rational functions—which is fair since it involves quite a few steps and a lot of shifting variables around. Before getting into the technical parts of solving expressions, I’d always make sure students understood their goals conceptually. But when I’d ask a student what a domain is and they would fail to give me a correct answer. Some would get within a ballpark, saying something about “the x’s that are good,” but the majority would stare blankly and, even after breaking down the question further, couldn’t even produce a guess. This was alarming since rational functions are taught about two thirds the way through the course while functions in general are a week two topic. Somehow a staggering amount of students got through most of the course without knowing the most basic definitions.

I’ve gotten this far without mentioning the elephant with a $500 billion dollar valuation in the room. ChatGPT is comically bad at algebra. It can get answers right a lot of the time, but it is still remarkable to me that students (even ignoring any ethical concerns) will still use a tool that is wrong sometimes instead of tools that are decades old that do exactly what they are asking ChatGPT to do. There are hundreds of step-by-step algebra solvers on the internet that may not be worth hundreds of billions of dollars but at least they don’t confidently give wrong answers or rely on slave labor. Regardless of this fact, it is clear that the only major useful application for ChatGPT is cheating on assignments. I think the most horrifying moment for me was when I saw a student using ChatGPT for an extremely simple question—something about the maximum of a given function on a graph—on a practice test for the final exam. At this point the student and I knew each other well enough that I could stop them and urge them to try it by themselves without being annoying. Sure enough, they got the correct answer thirty seconds later. They knew how to solve the problem, but using ChatGPT became an automatic response—preternaturally cutting off any semblance of critical thinking or problem solving. This instance was deeply disturbing and, just from casually surveying computer screens while walking around the room, far from uncommon.

So how did so many students get through the course without knowing the material? It wasn’t just ChatGPT since most students passed the final and it wasn’t just cheating since truly exhaustive measures were taken to prevent any scandals. Even without these tricks, students could find correct answers.

I remember a time I was tutoring a student who was struggling with a pair of questions about some point on a graph. The questions asked for 1) the value of the function at that point and 2) the input value for that given point. The student was confused on whether or not the first question was asking for the x-value or the y-value. I, trying to be a good tutor, asked a follow up question about functions and inputs and, after enough coaxing, got the student to conclude that the first question was about the y-value and the second was about the x-value. The first question was notably longer than the second and the student said, “I’ll just remember that the question with more words is about the y-value since it will be the same format for the exam.” This was notably a bad strategy for a variety of reasons, chief among them being that it bypasses any need to actually understand what a function is by just memorizing the pattern of the exam. I briefly urged against this idea, but it was extremely close to the exam and I wasn’t going to strongly dissuade any tricks that may help a student pass. Also that was the second time I had heard that exact same strategy from a student for that question and I was too dumbfounded to protest.

There lies an answer to how students who don’t really understand the material are able to pass the final exam: they simply memorized enough patterns. I watched a majority of students skip the learning page for a topic on ALEKS, go immediately to the first question, then click the “get help” button to see how to solve it. Students got through the course just by looking at the question and recalling the random pushing of symbols around on the page that results in some meaningless correct answer—all without any intuitive understanding of what they are doing or why. Math to them is reduced to practicing hundreds of questions to forcibly mush some algorithm into their heads. The second there is any new material—say the lesson on the domain of rational functions—the process has to be started all over again. The material never builds upon itself and students never learn how to solve problems that aren’t phrased in the exact same way ALEKS teaches. If the course didn’t have infinite examples to try, these students would never pass. There wouldn’t be enough data to recognize the pattern.

That last sentence makes the parallel I’m about to draw obvious. The way these students learn math is almost exactly the same way a large language model (LLM) “learns”—which, needless to say, isn’t really learning at all. Nothing new or interesting can be created by learning this way. Not that all college algebra students will go on to explore the frontiers of math, but they are being deprived of the ability to think critically; to take a problem and translate it into rigorous language; to ask the series of simple follow-up questions that forge the path to a complex answer in a fundamentally human process we call reason. My job on the days when I could lead people through that reasoning were genuine sources of pride and joy. But there are only so many students I could help and so few students that were interested in learning in this way, so most days it sucked. It felt inhumane to pace around a room full of students who were forced to sit and memorize patterns that are devoid of meaning to answer questions devoid of purpose. It is genuinely heartbreaking to watch these students cast aside the defining skills of humanity to solve problems like an LLM—sometimes even skirting that duty altogether by offloading the entirety of the work to an actual LLM. ­

To be absolutely clear: this isn’t really the fault of the students. Students are forced to pay tens-of-thousands of dollars to take a class that forces them learn too much material far too quickly so they pass a single end-of-year exam which will determine whether or not they can move on to other courses. Most students view college as a job training program or, worse, as a set of arbitrary hoops to jump through in order to receive a piece of paper that allows them to get a job that pays well enough that they may be able to afford to eat or have kids or retire some day. Diplomas are a commodity and, in a commodity society, there is no reason to value anything other than a final result: an answer, a passed exam, a college degree, or a sufficient salary. This makes AI appealing since it produces the platonic ideal of a commodity—the end result and absolutely nothing else. Add in the country’s plummeting literacy rate, chronic deficits in attention spans, attacks on education funding, and the infinitely complex reality of individuals seeking meaning and self-actualization in their early adulthood while also interfacing with, and being constructed by, all the aforementioned problems and you’ve got an overwhelming nightmare.

I’ve been trying to figure out how to conclude for the last few days, finally just now sitting and writing on my porch enjoying the new year, but there isn’t really a major takeaway from this other than just an acknowledgment that institutional failures abound in every direction. I have proposed solutions for all these problems—stuff about treating people with dignity regardless of their contribution to the GDP and shifting our societies values away from products and individualism towards the beauty of process and collectivism and so on—but I don’t think they really matter. I can’t change any of these institutions. The only thing I can do is exactly what I did as a tutor: lead people through my own process of thinking and hope they arrive to the same conclusion. The idea I hope to arrive at is that world isn’t built for us, which is why we need to be mindful and patient with ourselves and others. In spite of everything, I really did have some earnest breakthroughs in teaching people math throughout the semester. I brought people back from frustrated tears and helped students rebuild their self-confidence after failed exams. Freshman year of college is a brutal, complicated time for a lot of people, so I like to imagine I helped people with more than just memorizing arbitrary patterns. This is the best I can do, and I’m proud of the work I’ve done.

“No one changes the world alone and no one doesn’t change it at all.”

-­-Hank Green

I hope everyone reading takes the time to enjoy the process of living and learning alongside everyone else. I am looking to be more patient and kind. I’m looking forward to 2026 and all that it holds and I hope you are too.

Thanks,

Miles

[^1]"But what about objects that are more than one color? Like zebras? That would be an input that returns two outputs: black and white. This violates our definition of a function” I hear you say. That can be solved easily with a brief redefinition of our function so that it doesn’t map to a color but to a set of colors that constitutes any object. Functions have no strict definition on what is or is not an input or output, so mapping objects to sets is totally fine. The image (the set of all possible outputs of our function), sometimes called the range, is then a set of sets of colors. If you want to go deeper, that image is a subset of the greater set of all possible sets of colors (regardless of there exists an object with those colors) called the co-domain. To make things more confusing, sometimes “range” is used to refer to both the co-domain and image, but most algebra courses use range interchangeably since the assumption is that our function maps the set of all real numbers to the set of all real numbers unless the function is just a random squiggle on a graph.
